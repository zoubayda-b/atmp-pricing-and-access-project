# Protocol
## Title:
A Comparative Policy Analysis of ATMP Pricing and Access: List Prices, Launch Sequencing, and Patient Access for Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products Across the United States, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom (2018–2024)

## Project Aim:
The aim of this project is to conduct a reproducible cross-country comparative policy analysis in R quantifying how publicly listed prices and the time from regulatory approval to reimbursed patient access for Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) vary across the United States, Germany, France, and England. All data processing and statistical analyses will be conducted in R using a fully reproducible workflow, with the analysis environment locked via `renv` and the software versions reported in the final report (R version, key package versions).

## Background
Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) encompassing cell therapies, gene therapies, and gene-edited cell therapies represent a rapidly expanding class of one-time, potentially curative treatments for serious and rare diseases. Since the first approvals in 2017 (tisagenlecleucel/Kymriah, axicabtagene ciloleucel/Yescarta, and voretigene neparvovec/Luxturna), more than a dozen ATMPs have received marketing authorisation from the FDA and/or the EMA, with list prices ranging from approximately $475,000 to over $3.5 million per course [@fda_cber; @ema_atmp]. These therapies pose unprecedented challenges for pricing, health technology assessment (HTA), and reimbursement systems that were largely designed for chronic, repeat-prescription pharmaceuticals.

The four jurisdictions in this analysis differ fundamentally in their institutional architecture for translating regulatory approval into patient access. The United States has no national HTA gate: FDA approval enables launch, but actual patient access depends on fragmented payer coverage decisions (CMS, commercial insurers, Medicaid). Germany's AMNOG system grants immediate market access at a manufacturer-set free price for approximately the first 12 months, after which the G-BA conducts a benefit assessment and the GKV-Spitzenverband negotiates a confidential rebate. France operates a sequential system in which the HAS Transparency Commission issues a medical-benefit opinion (SMR/ASMR), the CEPS negotiates a reimbursable price, and formal reimbursement follows; earlier access may nonetheless occur via temporary-use authorisations (ATU). England relies on NICE technology appraisal, with a statutory duty for NHS England to fund recommended technologies within 90 days of guidance publication. Following Brexit, UK authorisations after January 2021 may follow MHRA independent assessment or reliance procedures rather than the EU centralised route.

These structural differences produce systematically different access timelines and price-transparency environments. Cross-country comparisons of ATMP pricing and access have been limited by the absence of a harmonised dataset linking regulatory milestones, HTA decisions, and list prices across jurisdictions with comparable definitions. Existing indicators, such as the EFPIA/IQVIA Patients W.A.I.T. Indicator, measure time to patient access but do not separately account for the heterogeneity in how "access" is operationalised across systems [@efpia_wait]. Moreover, list prices; the only systematically observable price concept are confounded by confidential rebates (Germany), negotiated discounts (France), and commercial access agreements (England), meaning that list-price comparisons capture the signalling function of pricing rather than true budget impact.

Therefore, the objective of this project is to build a harmonised dataset and conduct a comparative policy analysis quantifying cross-country variation in ATMP list prices (adjusted for inflation and purchasing-power differences) and in the time from regulatory approval to reimbursed patient access, while transparently documenting where the comparison is and is not valid.

## Research Question
How do publicly listed prices and the time from regulatory approval to reimbursed patient access for ATMPs vary across the United States, Germany, France, and England, and to what extent can observed variation be attributed to differences in HTA and reimbursement architecture?

Secondary questions: (1) What is the magnitude of cross-country list-price differences for the same ATMP, after purchasing-power-parity (PPP) and inflation adjustment? (2) How long does it take from EMA, FDA, or MHRA approval to first reimbursed availability in each country, and which countries are consistently faster or slower? (3) Is there an association between list price and launch delay (the "budget-shock" hypothesis)? (4) What happens to ATMPs that receive regulatory authorisation but fail to achieve sustainable reimbursement, and what does this reveal about the limits of current access frameworks?

## Analytical Framework
### Scope
The unit of analysis is the drug × country pair (maximum 17 drugs × 4 countries = 68 observations; fewer in practice due to missing data). The regulatory-approval window is 2017–2024 (to include the foundational 2017 approvals), and the cross-country analytical comparison window is 2018–2024.

### Population (Products)
Commercially marketed ATMPs classified as cell therapy, gene therapy, or gene-edited cell therapy under EU Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007 or FDA CBER designation, that received marketing authorisation from the FDA and/or EMA from 2017 onward and for which at least one of list price, regulatory date, or HTA date is observable.

### Setting (Countries)
The United States, Germany, France, and England. The analysis covers NHS England/NICE only; Scotland (SMC) and Wales (AWTTC/AWMSG) run separate HTA processes and are excluded. Regulators, HTA bodies, and reimbursement mechanisms differ by country (FDA/CBER with no national HTA gate and multi-payer reimbursement in the US; G-BA under AMNOG with statutory-health-insurance negotiation in Germany; HAS and CEPS with Assurance Maladie reimbursement in France; MHRA/EMA with NICE appraisal and NHS England commissioning in England).

### Outcomes
- **List price** (primary price outcome): public list price in local currency at or near launch, adjusted to constant-year USD via CPI deflation and PPP conversion, and expressed as a cross-country price index relative to the US.
- **Launch delay** (primary access outcome): time from regulatory approval to first reimbursed patient access (`delay_regulatory_to_access`), in days.
- **HTA ratings** (descriptive): G-BA additional-benefit rating (Germany), HAS SMR/ASMR (France), and NICE recommendation (England), summarised descriptively and coded ordinally for exploratory within-country correlation.
- **Access status** (descriptive): whether each product achieved sustainable reimbursement, was commercially withdrawn, or was not submitted/approved in a given country.

## Eligibility Criteria
### Inclusion Criteria
- Products that received marketing authorisation from the FDA (CBER) and/or the EMA (CHMP/EC), and/or the MHRA, from 2017 onward.
- Classified as a cell therapy, gene therapy, or gene-edited cell therapy under EU Regulation (EC) No 1394/2007 or FDA CBER designation.
- Commercially marketed (not solely a clinical-trial or compassionate-use product).
- At least one of list price, regulatory date, or HTA date observable in a primary or authoritative source.

### Exclusion Criteria
- Products first approved before 2017 (e.g., sipuleucel-T, 2010; limbal stem cells, 2015; talimogene laherparepvec, 2015). Documented as historical context but not in the analytical dataset.
- Tissue-engineered products without gene modification (e.g., darvadstrocel, MACI, Stratagraft), whose pricing dynamics differ fundamentally from gene and CAR-T therapies.
- Cord blood / haematopoietic progenitor cell products (e.g., Hemacord, Allocord), which are not novel ATMPs in the policy-relevant sense.
- Products for which no regulatory date, HTA date, or price can be verified from any authoritative source for any of the four countries.
- Products regulated primarily as diagnostic devices rather than therapeutics.

## Data Sources and Search Strategy
### Sources
Data will be drawn from primary and authoritative sources, prioritised in a fixed hierarchy: (1) regulatory authorities — EMA European Public Assessment Reports, the European Commission, FDA approval letters and Drugs@FDA, and the MHRA; (2) national HTA and reimbursement institutions — NICE, HAS, CEPS, G-BA, IQWiG, and GKV-Spitzenverband; (3) government and official legislation — EUR-Lex, Légifrance (JORF), and national government publications; (4) international organisations — OECD and WHO for PPP and CPI factors; (5) manufacturer primary sources — launch announcements, pricing press releases, and investor communications; (6) academic literature; and (7) specialist pharmaceutical trade press (e.g., Fierce Pharma, Endpoints News) for cross-checking only. A tier-7 source will not be used when a tier-1–3 source is reasonably available. For every important observation, both a primary source and at least one verification source will be recorded where feasible, with `source_primary`, `source_url`, `date_accessed`, and, where feasible, `source_secondary` fields. Where two credible sources disagree, the discrepancy will be flagged, investigated, and the resolution documented.

### Country and Period Anchors
Regulatory dates are anchored to FDA approval (US), EMA EC decision (EU pre-2021 / UK pre-2021), or MHRA approval (UK post-2021). The dual-anchor approach for the UK records which anchor was used (`regulatory_anchor`), reflecting post-Brexit regulatory divergence. The data extraction cutoff is recorded per source for reproducibility.

## Data Extraction
### Variables
A standardised data extraction form will be developed and piloted before formal extraction. For each drug × country pair the following will be recorded: product identifiers (INN, brand name, ATMP modality, manufacturer); regulatory milestones (regulatory date and anchor); HTA variables (HTA body, decision date, rating, recommendation type); reimbursement and access dates; commercial launch date; and price variables (list price in local currency, price basis, price year, price confidentiality flag).

### Price Definition
The four jurisdictions do not have a single comparable "price." Distinct concepts: the US Wholesale Acquisition Cost (WAC; the manufacturer's published list price to wholesalers), the EU ex-factory list price, the reimbursement price, and the net transaction price — must not be mixed. The primary price variable collected is the public list price in local currency at or near launch (`list_price_lcu`), with a `price_basis` field documenting what the price represents (WAC, ex-factory, CEPS-set, or NICE-stated) and a `price_confidential` flag where the true transaction price is hidden. Per-country operationalisation: US WAC at launch (with per-course price computed as WAC × units per course); the German ex-factory list price (*Lauer-Taxe*) during the AMNOG free-pricing period; the CEPS-set manufacturer price (*prix fabricant*) where published in the JORF, coded confidential for hospital-only products; and the NICE-evaluated list price where stated, otherwise confidential. This study collects list prices, not net prices; confidential rebates, negotiated discounts, and commercial agreements mean true transaction prices are lower and unobserved. This is a defined limitation: the list price captures the signalling function of pricing but understates the discounting that determines real budget impact.

### Access and Launch-Delay Definition
A single "launch delay" variable is conceptually invalid because it forces four non-comparable institutional processes into one measure. The dataset therefore records five distinct date variables per drug × country pair: `regulatory_date` (all countries); `hta_date` (DE, FR, UK); `reimbursement_date` (DE, FR, UK); `commercial_launch_date` (where known); and `first_access_date` (the primary analytical endpoint). The primary launch-delay comparison variable is `delay_regulatory_to_access = first_access_date − regulatory_date` (days). The `first_access_date` is operationalised as: FDA approval date (US); EMA EC decision date, i.e. day 1 of the free-pricing period (Germany); CEPS price decision date (France); and NICE guidance publication date (England). Each operationalisation carries a known, asymmetric bias: the US and Germany overstate access speed, France understates it (ignoring ATU early access), and England overstates it (ignoring the 90-day NHS funding lag). These biases are documented and addressed via sensitivity analysis. Because the US `first_access_date` equals the FDA approval date, the US `delay_regulatory_to_access` is always zero and the US is excluded from the cross-country launch-delay comparison (included in price comparisons only); an alternative US access date (e.g., first CMS National Coverage Determination) is used where available. Germany's delay is also approximately zero because its AMNOG system grants immediate market access at a free price, with benefit assessment and negotiation occurring in parallel a genuine finding rather than an artefact. The launch-delay definition mirrors the logic of the EFPIA/IQVIA Patients W.A.I.T. Indicator, cited as a methodological precedent rather than a data source.

### HTA Variables
HTA variables are collected per country: Germany - G-BA additional-benefit rating (`de_benefit`), G-BA resolution date, and rebate-agreement date; France - SMR, ASMR, HAS Transparency Commission opinion date, and CEPS price decision date; England - NICE recommendation (recommended/optimised/not recommended), guidance date, appraisal type (HST or TA), and commercial-arrangement status; the US has no national HTA body. HTA ratings are coded ordinally for exploratory correlation: France ASMR I–V mapped to 5–1; Germany *Zusatznutzen* (*Erheblich* = 4, *Beträchtlich* = 3, *Gering* = 2, *Nicht quantifizierbar* = 2, *Kein Zusatznutzen* = 0, *Weniger als zweckmässige Vergleichstherapie* = −1); and England recommended = 2, optimised = 1, not recommended = 0. *Nicht quantifizierbar* (benefit demonstrated but not quantifiable) is placed at the same level as *Gering*, since it is not worse than a minor benefit. ASMR, *Zusatznutzen*, and NICE ratings are not directly comparable across systems; ordinal coding enables exploratory within-country correlation but not valid cross-country comparison of HTA ratings.

### Price-Adjustment Methodology
Prices are transformed in a strict, documented, reversible sequence: CPI deflation is applied first in the source country's local currency (using OECD all-items CPI, chained to a 2024 = 100 index), then PPP conversion to constant-year USD (using OECD GDP-based PPP for 2024, in local currency per USD), and finally a price index is computed as `(country constant-USD price / US constant-USD price) × 100`. Applying both CPI and PPP is not double-adjustment because they address different dimensions: PPP removes cross-sectional price-level differences between countries, and CPI removes temporal price-level change within a country. The original approach of applying country-specific CPI after PPP conversion was a conceptual error (it mixed inflation rates across currencies); the corrected pipeline deflates in local currency before conversion. For every transformed price the dataset retains the original price, currency, price year, PPP factor, CPI index, the intermediate PPP-adjusted value, and the final adjusted price, so the full transformation is reproducible.

### Missing-Data Rules
No data is fabricated. Missing values are coded explicitly with a documented reason. Products not approved in a country have all country-specific variables coded `NA` (structural missingness). Products withdrawn from a country retain pre-withdrawal data with the withdrawal date and reason recorded. Confidential prices have `list_price_lcu = NA`, `price_confidential = TRUE`, and a free-text note. Pending HTA assessments are coded `NA` with status documented. OECD API failures yield `NA` price factors and the pipeline degrades gracefully. Where two sources disagree and the discrepancy cannot be resolved, the more authoritative source wins (per the source hierarchy); if neither is clearly more authoritative, the value is coded uncertain and both sources documented. A smaller, well-validated dataset is preferred to a larger dataset containing questionable observations.

## Risk of Bias and Data Quality
The quality of each observation will be assessed qualitatively using the source hierarchy, with every observation carrying primary and (where feasible) secondary source attribution. The principal threats to validity are not within-study bias in the clinical-trial sense but source reliability, price-transparency limitations (confidential net prices), and the non-comparability of country-specific access endpoints. These are documented per variable and summarised in the report rather than scored with a formal instrument.

## Statistical Analysis
### Descriptive Analysis
Descriptive statistics: N, median, interquartile range (IQR), and range (min–max)  will be reported per country via `gtsummary`. Means will not be relied upon: with fewer than 20 products and prices spanning roughly $475,000 to $3.5 million, the mean is pulled upward by outliers. Missingness will be reported explicitly (number and percentage of missing observations per variable per country).

### Price Comparison
The cross-country price index (`(country constant-USD price / US constant-USD price) × 100`) will be computed per drug × country pair, with the distribution (median, IQR, range) reported per country (50 = half the US price; 100 = equal; 150 = 50% above). The index is undefined for EU-only products without a US price; these are retained in the dataset but excluded from index calculation.

### Launch-Delay Comparison
Median, IQR, and range of `delay_regulatory_to_access` will be reported per country and visualised as a ridgeline plot (`ggridges`) to show distribution shape. The US is excluded (delay = 0 by definition) and reported separately using an alternative definition where data permit.

### Correlation Analysis
Spearman rank correlations (`cor.test(method = "spearman")`) will be computed between `price_constant_usd` and `delay_regulatory_to_access` (the "budget-shock" hypothesis) and between the ordinal HTA rating and price within each country's system. The Spearman ρ and its confidence interval (Fisher z-transform or bootstrap) will be reported, not the p-value alone. Correlation does not establish causality; with the available paired observations the analysis is exploratory and the confidence interval is wide. A large ρ with a wide CI crossing zero is not evidence of no association but of insufficient power.

### Regression
No regression modelling will be performed. With approximately 17 drugs and at most four observations per drug, any multivariable regression would be severely underpowered, prone to overfitting, and uninterpretable. This decision is stated explicitly in the report.

### Sensitivity Analyses
Sensitivity analyses will evaluate robustness to key methodological choices: (1) alternative German access date using AMNOG negotiation completion (time to price certainty rather than first availability); (2) alternative English access date adding the 90-day NHS funding lag; (3) alternative French access date using the HAS avis date instead of the CEPS date; (4) alternative price normalisation using actual-individual-consumption PPP instead of GDP-based PPP; (5) alternative price reference using a four-country median instead of US = 100; (6) excluding EU-withdrawn products (Zynteglo, Skysona); (7) excluding EU-only products without a US price; (8) per-course price instead of per-unit WAC for the US; and (9) an alternative CPI base year (2020). For each analysis, whether the main conclusions (rankings, correlation direction and magnitude) change will be reported; if they do not, the findings are robust, and if they do, the finding is flagged as sensitive to that choice.

## Limitations
These limitations are stated in the report and not treated as afterthoughts. **Structural:** this study collects list prices, not net prices, so confidential rebates and discounts mean true transaction prices are lower and unobserved, and the list-to-net gap varies by country and product. The `first_access_date` is operationalised differently in each country, with asymmetric biases (US and Germany overstate access speed; France understates it; England overstates it), so cross-country launch-delay comparisons should be interpreted accordingly. The US is excluded from launch-delay comparison because its delay is zero by definition. The England-only scope excludes Scotland and Wales, whose decisions and timelines may differ. Post-2021 UK products may follow a different regulatory pathway than pre-2021 products, introducing a discontinuity in the UK time series. **Statistical:** with approximately 17 drugs and at most 68 observations (fewer in practice), statistical power is very limited and all analyses are descriptive or exploratory; no causal inference is attempted. Spearman correlations have very wide confidence intervals and a non-significant result does not prove absence of association. No regression modelling is performed, so the analysis cannot control for confounders such as disease rarity, modality, or manufacturer size. HTA ratings are not cross-country comparable. **Data-quality:** prices are captured at or near launch and subsequent revisions are not tracked; PPP and CPI factors depend on OECD API retrieval, which, if unavailable, prevents price normalisation; "first reimbursed access" is operationalised via decision dates and real-world administration lag is not captured; and many ATMPs are hospital-administered with procurement prices that may differ from published list prices and may not be centrally recorded.

## Reporting Standard
The final report will transparently document the data sources, transformation pipeline, and all analytical decisions, including where the cross-country comparison is and is not valid. A data-quality report will flag missing PPP/CPI factors and confidential prices. All tables and figures (price-index distributions, launch-delay ridgeline plots, correlation scatterplots) will be generated programmatically from the analysis scripts.

## Reproducibility
Analyses will be conducted in R, with package versions locked via `renv::snapshot()` and the resulting lockfile committed to the repository. The R version and key package versions (`gtsummary`, `ggridges`) used for the final analysis will be reported in the final report. All data extraction and analysis scripts will be numbered and version-controlled in the project repository.
